# THE COMPLETE THERAPY ROADMAP
## Which Therapies Work, When to Use Them, and How to Build Your Child's Dream Team
### A Plain-English Guide for Parents of Children with Autism

## WELCOME

After the autism diagnosis, every parent faces the same overwhelming question: *What do I do now?*

The world of autism therapies is enormous, confusing, and full of competing claims. You've probably heard about ABA, speech therapy, occupational therapy, DIR/Floortime, RDI, social skills groups, sensory integration, play therapy, music therapy, animal-assisted therapy — and the list goes on.

Some of these approaches are backed by decades of research. Others have minimal evidence. Many cost thousands of dollars per month. Knowing which ones to prioritize, how to sequence them, and what to look for in providers can save you years of wasted time and tens of thousands of dollars.

This guide gives you the complete picture — evidence ratings, typical costs, what good providers look like, and a customized framework for building your child's therapy team.

## PART 1: THE RESEARCH LANDSCAPE

### How We Evaluate Therapies

**Evidence Levels (used throughout this guide):**
- ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Strong Evidence:** Multiple randomized controlled trials (RCTs), peer-reviewed research, broad scientific consensus
- ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Good Evidence:** Multiple studies showing effectiveness, though not all are RCTs
- ⭐⭐⭐ **Promising:** Some studies showing effectiveness, more research needed
- ⭐⭐ **Emerging:** Early studies or case reports; may help some children
- ⭐ **Insufficient Evidence:** Anecdotal only, or studies show no benefit

### The Most Important Framework: What Does Your Child Need?

Before choosing therapies, assess your child's current needs across five domains:

| Domain | Questions to Ask |
|---|---|
| **Communication** | Can they make requests? Express wants/needs? Have back-and-forth conversation? |
| **Social** | Do they notice others? Seek interaction? Engage in play with peers? |
| **Behavior** | Are there interfering behaviors? Self-injury? Meltdowns? Elopement? |
| **Daily Living** | Can they dress, eat, use the bathroom independently? |
| **Sensory/Motor** | Are they hypersensitive/hyposensitive? Fine motor delays? Coordination issues? |

Your therapy team should address each domain where your child has significant needs. Most children with autism need a combination of approaches.

## PART 2: THE CORE THERAPIES

### Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA)

**Evidence Level:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Highest evidence base for autism)
**Best for:** All ages, especially early intervention; behavior challenges; skill acquisition
**Typical frequency:** 20–40 hours/week (early intensive) or 10–15 hours/week (supplemental)
**Typical cost:** $120–$250/hour; often covered by insurance and Medicaid
**Who provides it:** Board Certified Behavior Analyst (BCBA) with Registered Behavior Technicians (RBTs)

**What ABA actually is:**
ABA uses principles of learning science to teach skills and reduce behaviors that interfere with learning and quality of life. Modern ABA is naturalistic, play-based, and focused on meaningful outcomes — not the rigid, drill-based approaches of the 1970s.

**Core ABA techniques:**
- **Discrete Trial Training (DTT):** Structured, repeated practice of specific skills
- **Natural Environment Teaching (NET):** Teaching skills in natural contexts (play, daily routines)
- **Verbal Behavior (VB):** Language-focused ABA using Skinner's analysis of verbal behavior
- **Pivotal Response Treatment (PRT):** Naturalistic, motivation-based approach
- **Early Start Denver Model (ESDM):** Research-backed early intervention model (0–5 years)

**What good ABA looks like:**
✓ Individualized, based on your child's specific goals
✓ Heavy use of natural environment teaching (not just table-based)
✓ Regular parent training — you learn to implement strategies at home
✓ Goals include social, communication, play, AND behavior
✓ Regular data collection and graph reviews
✓ Frequent team meetings including parents
✓ BCBA with at least 2 years of experience with autism

**Red flags in an ABA program:**
✗ No parent training component
✗ Only table-based, drill-format work
✗ Aversive techniques (punishment-heavy approaches)
✗ No regular data review or program updates
✗ RBTs doing most decision-making without BCBA involvement
✗ Cookie-cutter program identical to other children

**How to evaluate ABA providers:**
1. Ask for their BCBA's experience and caseload size
2. Ask to see sample data and program books
3. Ask about parent training frequency and format
4. Ask how they determine when goals are mastered
5. Request references from current families

**Getting ABA funded:**
- **Insurance:** Under ACA, most plans covering children must cover ABA. The Autism Insurance Reform Laws have passed in all 50 states — your insurer cannot refuse ABA categorically.
- **Medicaid:** Covered as a medically necessary service in most states
- **IEP:** Schools can provide ABA — push for it if private ABA is not accessible

### Speech-Language Therapy (SLT)

**Evidence Level:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
**Best for:** Communication at all levels — from non-speaking to conversation development
**Typical frequency:** 2–5x/week, 45–60 minutes/session
**Typical cost:** $150–$300/hour; covered by insurance and school IEP
**Who provides it:** Speech-Language Pathologist (SLP) — look for CCC-SLP credential

**Speech therapy for autism covers:**
- **Functional communication:** Making requests, protesting, commenting
- **Expressive language:** Sentence structure, vocabulary, narrative
- **Receptive language:** Following directions, understanding concepts
- **Pragmatic/social language:** Turn-taking, topic maintenance, reading social cues
- **Articulation:** Clarity of speech sounds
- **AAC (Augmentative and Alternative Communication):** For non-speaking or minimally verbal children

**Non-speaking and minimally verbal children:** AAC should be introduced as early as possible. Research shows AAC does NOT reduce speech — it supports language development. Common AAC systems:
- PECS (Picture Exchange Communication System)
- High-tech devices: Proloquo2Go (iPad app), LAMP Words for Life, TouchChat
- Low-tech boards and books

**Finding a strong autism SLP:**
- Look for PROMPT-trained SLPs (for children with motor speech difficulties)
- Look for SLPs with AAC experience for non-speaking children
- Ask about their approach to pragmatic/social language
- Ask how they coordinate with ABA and home

### Occupational Therapy (OT)

**Evidence Level:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Strong for sensory integration, fine motor, daily living skills)
**Best for:** Sensory processing, fine motor skills, self-care, handwriting
**Typical frequency:** 1–3x/week, 45–60 minutes/session
**Typical cost:** $150–$250/hour; covered by insurance and school IEP

**What OT addresses for autism:**
- **Sensory Processing:** Hyper/hyposensitivity to touch, sound, light, movement, taste, smell
- **Fine Motor Skills:** Handwriting, scissors use, manipulating objects
- **Daily Living Skills (ADLs):** Dressing, grooming, eating, hygiene
- **Visual-Motor Integration:** Eye-hand coordination, spatial awareness
- **Self-Regulation:** Identifying and managing sensory overload, emotional states

**Sensory Diet:** Your OT should develop a personalized "sensory diet" — a schedule of sensory activities throughout the day that help your child maintain optimal arousal. Bring this to school and implement it at home.

**Sensory Integration vs. Sensory Strategies:**
- SI therapy (formal): Done in a specialized clinic with suspended equipment, ball pits, etc.
- Sensory strategies: Tools and techniques you use at home and school (weighted blankets, fidgets, compression vests, movement breaks)

**What to bring to OT evaluations:**
- List of specific sensory challenges (sounds, textures, transitions)
- Feeding challenges (texture aversions, food refusals)
- Fine motor concerns (pencil grip, fastening buttons)
- Daily living skill gaps (still needs help dressing at age 8?)

### Physical Therapy (PT)

**Evidence Level:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐
**Best for:** Gross motor development, coordination, core strength, mobility
**Typical frequency:** 1–2x/week for children with significant gross motor delays
**Typical cost:** $150–$250/hour; covered by insurance and school IEP

Many children with autism have gross motor delays not caused by autism itself but by low muscle tone (hypotonia) or other co-occurring conditions. PT addresses:
- Walking, running, jumping milestones
- Balance and coordination
- Sports and recreation participation
- Core strength
- Gait abnormalities

**PT is sometimes overlooked** for children with autism if behavior/communication needs are prominent. If your child has gross motor delays, request a PT evaluation through the school or privately.

## PART 3: NATURALISTIC AND DEVELOPMENTAL APPROACHES

### DIR/Floortime

**Evidence Level:** ⭐⭐⭐ (Good evidence for relationship development)
**Best for:** Building emotional connections, motivation-based learning, relationship development
**Philosophy:** Emphasizes following the child's lead and building from the "bottom up" — emotional connection → communication → thinking

**How it works:** Therapist or parent gets on the floor and follows the child's lead in play, expanding and challenging the child's interactions at their current developmental level.

**Strengths:** Excellent for building emotional connection and motivated communication. Often preferred by families who find traditional ABA too structured.

**Limitations:** Less evidence for discrete skill acquisition compared to ABA; requires intensive, consistent implementation.

**Best used as:** A complement to ABA and SLT, particularly for emotional/relationship development and generalization of skills.

---

### Relationship Development Intervention (RDI)

**Evidence Level:** ⭐⭐⭐
**Best for:** Dynamic intelligence, flexible thinking, social relationships
**Focus:** Building guided participation between parents and children to develop flexible, experience-based thinking

RDI targets the underlying cognitive processes behind autism (dynamic intelligence, experience sharing) rather than just surface behaviors. Parent training is central to the model.

---

### PLAY Project (Play and Language for Autistic Youngsters)

**Evidence Level:** ⭐⭐⭐
**Best for:** Young children (0–6), early communication, parent-implemented
**Format:** Parent training model with monthly consultations; parents implement 15–25 hours/week at home

A more accessible, parent-centered alternative to clinic-based intensive therapy for some families.

## PART 4: SOCIAL SKILLS INTERVENTIONS

### Social Skills Groups

**Evidence Level:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐
**Best for:** School-age children with sufficient verbal skills; generalization of social interaction
**Format:** Small groups of 4–8 children, therapist-facilitated, structured social practice

**What to look for in a social skills group:**
- Clinician-led with structured curriculum (PEERS, Social Thinking are research-based)
- Mixed group including some neurotypical peers (pure autism-only groups have limits)
- Parent coaching component — you need to practice at home too
- Real-world skill application (not just scripted practice)

**PEERS (Program for the Education and Enrichment of Relational Skills):** UCLA-developed, one of the strongest evidence-based social skills programs for adolescents and young adults. Available in many settings.

**Social Thinking (Michelle Garcia Winner):** Curriculum and framework for understanding social cognition — widely used in schools and therapy settings.

---

### Video Modeling

**Evidence Level:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐
**Best for:** Teaching social skills, daily living skills, communication
**How it works:** Child watches videos of correct behavior being modeled, then practices the skill

Can be implemented by parents using simple recordings. Research shows high effectiveness for teaching a variety of skills.

## PART 5: COMPLEMENTARY AND ALTERNATIVE APPROACHES

### Music Therapy

**Evidence Level:** ⭐⭐⭐
**Best for:** Communication, social engagement, emotional regulation
Music therapy uses musical activities to address non-musical goals. Particularly effective for children who are highly responsive to music (common in autism).

### Animal-Assisted Therapy

**Evidence Level:** ⭐⭐
**Best for:** Emotional regulation, social motivation, anxiety reduction
Horses (equine-assisted therapy/hippotherapy), dogs, and other animals can provide motivating contexts for skill development. Often covered by some insurance or Medicaid waivers.

### Sensory Integration Therapy

**Evidence Level:** ⭐⭐⭐ (for specific sensory goals)
Formal sensory integration therapy in a specialized OT setting.

### Biomedical Interventions
**Evidence Level:** Highly variable — discuss with a developmental pediatrician
- Special diets (GFCF, ketogenic): Some families report improvements; limited research
- Supplements: Omega-3, B6/Mg, melatonin (for sleep) — some evidence; discuss with doctor
- GI treatment: Many children with autism have GI issues that when treated improve functioning

**Important:** Always discuss biomedical interventions with your child's physician. Some promoted "treatments" (MMS, chelation) are dangerous and should be avoided entirely.

## PART 6: BUILDING YOUR CHILD'S THERAPY TEAM

### The Core Team (Most Children Need All Three)
1. **BCBA + ABA program** — behavior, skill acquisition, intensive support
2. **Speech-Language Pathologist** — communication
3. **Occupational Therapist** — sensory, fine motor, self-care

### Extended Team (Add as Needed)
4. **Physical Therapist** — if gross motor delays present
5. **Social skills group therapist** — for school-age children with verbal skills
6. **Developmental Pediatrician** — medical oversight, medication if needed
7. **Child Psychologist** — anxiety, mood, behavioral health
8. **Nutritionist** — if significant feeding challenges

### Coordinating Your Team

**The most common and costly mistake:** Therapies work in silos. Your child's BCBA, SLP, OT, and school teacher never talk to each other.

**What to do:**
- Request a joint team meeting at least quarterly
- Ask each provider to share written reports with the others (with your consent)
- Create a shared "home program" document that all providers contribute to
- Keep a communication notebook that travels with your child

### Provider Interview Questions

Before starting with any new provider, ask:
1. How many years have you worked with children with autism? What ages?
2. What's your theoretical approach/model?
3. How do you involve parents in the therapy process?
4. How do you coordinate with other members of the team?
5. How do you measure and report progress?
6. What would you want me to do at home between sessions?
7. What's your approach when a child isn't making progress?

### Red Flags in Any Provider
- Dismisses or doesn't communicate with other team members
- No parent involvement or training
- Can't explain their approach clearly to a non-expert
- No data collection or progress measurement
- Promises or guarantees specific outcomes
- Discourages you from seeking other opinions

## PART 7: THERAPY SCHEDULING AND MANAGING INTENSITY

### Early Intervention (Ages 0–5): Intensity Matters

Research consistently shows that **earlier and more intensive** intervention produces better outcomes. The "magic number" of 20–40 hours/week of intervention comes from early ABA research and has been repeatedly supported.

**Practical reality:** 40 hours/week of ABA is not achievable for most families due to cost, therapist availability, and child fatigue. **An achievable intensive program looks like:**
- 15–25 hours/week of direct ABA
- 3x/week speech therapy
- 2x/week OT
- Active parent implementation at home (this is why parent training is non-negotiable)

Total: 20–35 "hours" counting parent-led interaction

**School hours count!** If your child's school IEP includes ABA support and speech/OT, those hours contribute to your total.

### School Age (5–12): Intensive + Targeted

By school age, the focus shifts from intensity to targeting specific deficit areas:
- Social skills become more important
- Generalization across environments is the goal
- Self-regulation and independence building takes priority

**Typical school-age therapy load:**
- ABA: 10–15 hrs/week (if behavior/skill gaps significant) OR transition to school-based support only
- Speech: 2–3x/week
- OT: 1–2x/week
- Social skills group: 1x/week

### Teen Years (12+): Independence and Transition

Therapy at this stage focuses on:
- Vocational skills
- Independent living skills
- Emotional regulation and mental health
- Community participation
- Transition to adult services
