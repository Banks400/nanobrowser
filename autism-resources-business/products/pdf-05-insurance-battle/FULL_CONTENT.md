## INTRODUCTION: THE SYSTEM IS DESIGNED TO WEAR YOU DOWN

Insurance companies deny claims as a business strategy. The actuarial reality is that if they deny enough claims, a significant percentage of families give up — saving the insurer money.

Your job is to not give up.

This guide gives you the knowledge to fight insurance denials effectively. You will learn exactly what to say, when to say it, and how to escalate until you win. Thousands of autism families fight and win these battles every year. You can too.

## PART 1: YOUR LEGAL RIGHTS

### Federal Laws That Protect You

**The Affordable Care Act (ACA)**
The ACA prohibits most health insurance plans from:
- Denying coverage for pre-existing conditions
- Imposing lifetime or annual dollar limits on essential health benefits
- Charging more for services covered under essential health benefits than the plan's cost-sharing

**Mental Health Parity and Addiction Equity Act (MHPAEA)**
Insurers cannot impose stricter limitations on mental health/behavioral health benefits than they apply to comparable medical/surgical benefits. ABA therapy is generally classified as a behavioral health benefit — insurers cannot apply special restrictions not applied to other therapies.

**State Autism Insurance Mandates**
All 50 states have passed autism insurance reform laws. These laws require:
- Coverage of autism diagnosis and treatment (including ABA)
- Prohibition on age limits (in most states)
- Prohibition on dollar caps (in most states)
- Specific coverage amounts per year in some states

**Your state's specific mandate:** Search "[Your State] autism insurance mandate 2024" or visit autismspeaks.org/state-advocacy

**Important limitations:**
- These mandates apply to fully-insured plans (typically employer plans bought through an insurer)
- Self-funded employer plans (ERISA plans) are governed by federal law, not state mandates
- Ask your HR department: "Is this plan fully insured or self-funded?" Self-funded plans may have different rules

## PART 2: THE PRE-AUTHORIZATION GAME

### Never Start Services Without Pre-Auth

Before starting ABA, speech, OT, or any other autism therapy, always:

1. Call member services and ask: "Is this service covered under my plan?"
2. Get a referral from your pediatrician/developmental pediatrician (often required)
3. Submit a pre-authorization request with supporting documentation
4. Get the approval in WRITING before services begin

**Pre-authorization documentation package:**
- Autism diagnosis report
- Letter of medical necessity from your treating physician or diagnosing clinician
- Treatment plan from the therapy provider (including recommended hours/week)
- Any prior treatment records and progress notes

### The Letter of Medical Necessity (Critical)

The single most powerful document in your insurance fight. It must:
- Come from a licensed physician (MD/DO) — preferably a developmental pediatrician or psychiatrist
- State the specific diagnosis (include DSM-5 codes: F84.0 for Level 1, F84.0 for Level 2/3 ASD)
- Specify the exact treatment requested (ABA, 30 hours/week)
- Explain WHY this treatment is medically necessary for THIS child
- Reference the scientific evidence (you can help your doctor draft this)

**Template to bring to your doctor:**

> This letter is to certify that [Child's Name], DOB [date], is my patient and has been diagnosed with Autism Spectrum Disorder (DSM-5 F84.0, Level [1/2/3]).
>
> [He/She] requires Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA) therapy, [X] hours per week, based on [his/her] individualized assessment and treatment plan developed by a Board Certified Behavior Analyst.
>
> ABA therapy is the most evidence-based treatment for autism, supported by the US Surgeon General, American Academy of Pediatrics, American Psychological Association, and decades of peer-reviewed research. Without this treatment, [Child's Name]'s functional abilities in communication, social interaction, and adaptive behavior are expected to decline/fail to develop appropriately.
>
> This treatment is medically necessary to prevent further deterioration and to enable [him/her] to function at the highest possible level.

## PART 3: UNDERSTANDING AND FIGHTING DENIALS

### Types of Denials and How to Fight Each

**Denial Type 1: "Not Medically Necessary"**
This is the most common denial. The insurer claims the service isn't medically necessary.

**Response strategy:**
1. Request the full explanation of the denial in writing, including the clinical criteria used
2. Ask for the insurer's specific medical necessity criteria for ABA therapy
3. Have your treating clinician write a rebuttal letter addressing those specific criteria
4. Request a peer-to-peer review (your doctor speaks directly with the insurer's medical reviewer)
5. Submit peer-reviewed research supporting your request (BCBA or SLP can help compile this)

**Peer-to-peer review is highly effective** — many denials reverse at this stage. Ask your doctor specifically: "Will you request a peer-to-peer review with the insurance company's medical director?"

---

**Denial Type 2: "Experimental/Investigational"**
The insurer claims the treatment is experimental. For ABA, this is simply wrong.

**Response strategy:**
- Cite the 50+ years of published research on ABA
- Reference the US Surgeon General's 1999 report on mental health (explicitly supports ABA for autism)
- Reference the American Academy of Pediatrics' endorsement of ABA
- Reference your state's autism insurance mandate (requires coverage of ABA)
- This denial almost never survives appeal when properly documented

---

**Denial Type 3: "Benefit Not Covered" / "Exclusion Applies"**
The insurer claims ABA isn't a covered benefit or an exclusion applies.

**Response strategy:**
- Request your complete benefit booklet (Summary Plan Description)
- If your plan does cover ABA (check your state mandate), request the specific plan language
- Cite your state's autism insurance mandate
- If this is an ERISA plan, file a complaint with the US Department of Labor

---

**Denial Type 4: "Hours Exceeded" / "Limit Reached"**
The insurer caps ABA hours below what your child needs.

**Response strategy:**
- Cite MHPAEA: ask what comparable medical benefit (e.g., physical therapy for a catastrophic injury) has the same hour limits
- Request the insurer's utilization management criteria in writing
- Challenge the limit as a quantitative treatment limitation that may violate parity law
- This is a strong legal argument — consider consulting a health insurance attorney

---

**Denial Type 5: "Not a Covered Provider"**
The provider isn't in-network, or their license type isn't covered.

**Response strategy:**
- Request an in-network exception if there are no qualified in-network providers in your area
- Document that you attempted to find in-network providers and couldn't (call 3–5, document their waitlists or inability to serve your child)
- Request the insurer provide a list of qualified in-network providers in your geographic area
- If they can't provide adequate in-network options, they typically must cover out-of-network at in-network rates

### The 4-Level Appeal Process

**Level 1: Internal Appeal (First-Level)**
- Must be filed within 60–180 days of denial (varies by plan — check your denial letter)
- Submit in writing with all supporting documentation
- The insurer must respond within 30 days (non-urgent) or 72 hours (urgent)
- Success rate: ~30–40%

**Level 2: Internal Appeal (Second-Level)**
- Some plans have a second internal level
- Request a different reviewer than who denied the first appeal
- Escalate documentation — more research, more clinician letters

**Level 3: External Independent Review**
- You have the right to have your case reviewed by an independent organization (IRO)
- IRO decisions are binding on the insurer in most states
- Request this BEFORE filing a regulatory complaint — it's often faster
- Success rate: ~40–50% for autism-related appeals
- Cost: Usually free or low-cost ($25–$75)

**Level 4: Regulatory Complaint + Legal Options**
- File a complaint with your state insurance commissioner
- File a complaint with your employer's HR department (for employer plans)
- File a complaint with the US Department of Labor (ERISA plans)
- Consult a health insurance attorney (many work on contingency for significant cases)
- File a complaint with the US Department of Health and Human Services

## PART 5: MANAGING ONGOING INSURANCE ISSUES

### Annual Re-Authorization

Most plans require annual re-authorization for ABA and other therapies. Start the process 6–8 weeks before the current authorization expires.

Re-authorization documentation package:
- Updated letter of medical necessity
- Progress report from BCBA/therapists showing progress AND ongoing need
- Updated treatment plan with next year's goals
- Any new assessments or evaluations

**Key: Document progress AND continuing need.** Insurers sometimes deny re-authorization if a child has made "too much progress." This is wrong — progress shows the treatment is working. Your rebuttal: "Progress demonstrates medical necessity of the treatment. Discontinuing would risk regression."

### Coordination of Benefits

If your child has both private insurance and Medicaid:
- Private insurance is primary
- Medicaid is secondary (covers what private insurance doesn't)
- Providers must bill primary first, then secondary
- You typically pay nothing out-of-pocket in this scenario

### Keeping Records

Create a dedicated insurance binder:
- All authorization letters and numbers
- All denial letters (with dates)
- All appeal submissions (with send dates and tracking)
- All phone calls (date, time, representative name, what was said)
- All correspondence with providers about insurance billing

**The phone call log is critical.** Get a reference number for every call. Say: "Can I please have a reference number for this call?" Insurance representatives can't later deny saying something if you have a reference number.

## PART 6: SCRIPTS FOR COMMON INSURANCE CONVERSATIONS

### Getting Pre-Authorization Approved

> "I'm calling to request prior authorization for Applied Behavior Analysis therapy, CPT code 97153, for my child who has a diagnosis of Autism Spectrum Disorder, ICD-10 F84.0. I have a letter of medical necessity from [Dr. Name]. Can you tell me what documentation I need to submit and your timeline for decision?"

### After a Denial: Requesting Appeal Information

> "I received a denial letter dated [date] with reference number [number]. I am formally requesting an internal appeal. Can you confirm your process, the deadline for submitting my appeal, and what documentation should be included?"

### Requesting Peer-to-Peer Review

> "I'm asking you to request a peer-to-peer review between my child's physician, Dr. [Name], and your medical director. This is regarding the denial of [service] dated [date]. Please have someone contact Dr. [Name] at [phone] to schedule the review."

### Challenging a Hours Cap

> "I'm calling about the authorization for [X] hours/week of ABA therapy, which I believe is inadequate for my child's medical needs. Under the Mental Health Parity Act, I'd like to know what comparable quantitative treatment limitations apply to other medical or surgical services covered under this plan."
