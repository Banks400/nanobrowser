### For Parents of Teens and Young Adults with Autism

## INTRODUCTION: THE CLIFF EVERYONE TALKS ABOUT

Parents of young adults with autism call it "the cliff" — the moment their child turns 21 or finishes school, and the systems and services that have supported them for years suddenly disappear.

The structured school day ends. The therapy sessions funded by the school end. The built-in community and routine end. For many families, it feels like stepping off a cliff into nothing.

It doesn't have to be this way.

The families who navigate this transition successfully have one thing in common: they started planning years — sometimes decades — in advance. This guide is that plan.

Whether your child is 12 or 20, there are critical steps you need to be taking right now to ensure a stable, meaningful life on the other side of the school years.

---

## PART 1: THE ADULT SERVICES LANDSCAPE

### What Changes at 21

When your child turns 21 (or graduates from high school, whichever comes last), several things change:

**What ends:**
- IDEA-funded special education and related services (IEP services)
- School-based ABA, speech, OT, PT
- School-based transition services
- Any services tied to their status as a school-age student

**What continues (if set up properly):**
- SSI benefits (typically increase since parental income is no longer counted)
- Medicaid and Medicaid waiver services
- ABLE account
- Any private therapy covered by insurance

**The core challenge:** Adult services are not an entitlement like school services. You cannot demand them as a right — they are funded programs with limited slots. This is why early planning is essential.

### The Adult Services Ecosystem

**Vocational Rehabilitation (VR)**
- State-run agency that helps people with disabilities prepare for, find, and maintain employment
- FREE services: job coaching, job placement, supported employment, assistive technology, post-secondary education support
- Apply at age 16 (earlier to build a relationship)

**Day Programs / Adult Day Services**
- Structured daily activity programs for adults with developmental disabilities
- Funded by Medicaid waivers
- Range from purely habilitative to supported employment to community-based activities
- Quality varies enormously — research and visit before committing

**Supported Employment**
- Job coach + employer working together to help person with disability perform competitive employment
- Goal: real jobs, real wages, real integration
- Funded by VR initially, then often by Medicaid waiver

**Residential Options**
- Living independently (with or without supports)
- Shared living / host home (living with a family or roommate who provides support)
- Group homes (4–6 residents, 24/7 staffing)
- ICF/ID (Intermediate Care Facility for Individuals with Intellectual Disabilities)
- Supported living (own apartment with staff support)

**Wait times for residential programs:** Often 5–10+ years. Get on lists now.

## PART 2: THE YEAR-BY-YEAR TIMELINE

### Ages 12–13: Build the Foundation

**What to do:**
- [ ] Have an honest assessment of your child's long-term support needs
- [ ] Research adult services in your area — what exists, what the wait times are
- [ ] Meet with your state's Developmental Disabilities (DD) agency to understand eligibility and process
- [ ] Ensure your child is registered with the DD agency (this often starts the clock on wait lists)
- [ ] Begin focused transition-oriented goal-setting in IEP (self-advocacy, daily living, vocational awareness)
- [ ] Open an ABLE account and begin contributing
- [ ] Begin estate planning consultations (what happens to your child if something happens to you?)

**Transition IEP Goals for this age:**
- Self-advocacy skills (knowing and articulating one's needs)
- Awareness of personal interests and potential vocational directions
- Money management basics
- Community participation skills (public transportation, community settings)

---

### Ages 14–15: Formalize the Transition Plan

**Legal requirement:** At age 16 (or earlier in many states), the IEP must include a formal Transition Plan.

**What to do:**
- [ ] Ensure the IEP includes a transition plan with postsecondary goals in:
  - Education/training
  - Employment
  - Independent living
- [ ] Begin vocational exploration: job shadows, informational interviews, interest inventories
- [ ] Enroll in vocational electives, life skills classes, community-based work experiences through school
- [ ] Contact Vocational Rehabilitation early — many VR agencies can open cases at 16
- [ ] Research post-secondary education options (vocational training, community college programs for students with disabilities, specialized transition programs)
- [ ] Contact estate planning attorney to discuss Special Needs Trust

**Key IEP question:** "Does this transition plan reflect what my child actually wants and needs for their future, or is it what's easiest for the school to provide?"

---

### Ages 16–17: Legal and Financial Setup

**The legal landscape at 18:** When your child turns 18, they become a legal adult in the eyes of the law — regardless of their cognitive or adaptive functioning level. This has major implications.

**Options for legal decision-making support:**

**1. Guardianship**
- Court grants you (the parent) legal authority to make decisions for your adult child
- Full guardianship vs. limited guardianship (only specific areas)
- Appropriate for individuals who cannot make informed decisions independently
- Removes the individual's legal rights — use the most limited form necessary
- Process: File petition in probate court, medical evaluations, legal representation recommended
- Cost: $1,500–$5,000+ in legal fees

**2. Supported Decision-Making**
- Less restrictive alternative to guardianship
- Your child retains legal rights but has a formal network of supporters who help them make decisions
- Recognized in many states now
- Best for individuals who can make decisions with support

**3. Durable Power of Attorney + Healthcare Proxy**
- Your child signs legal documents giving you authority in specific areas
- Requires sufficient understanding to sign the documents
- Less formal than guardianship, preserves more rights
- Good option for higher-functioning individuals

**4. Representative Payee**
- SSA-appointed person who manages SSI funds on behalf of the recipient
- If you'll be managing your child's SSI, you need to be named as representative payee

**What to do at ages 16–17:**
- [ ] Consult a special needs attorney about guardianship vs. alternatives
- [ ] Begin guardianship process if appropriate (takes several months)
- [ ] Create/update your will with provisions for your child's inheritance
- [ ] Establish a Special Needs Trust (SNT)
- [ ] Review and update life insurance (fund the SNT)
- [ ] Ensure child is registered to vote (if appropriate) — civic participation matters

---

### Special Needs Trust (Critical Financial Planning)

A Special Needs Trust (SNT) allows you to leave an inheritance for your child without disqualifying them from SSI and Medicaid.

**The problem without a SNT:** If you leave money directly to your child with a disability, assets above $2,000 will disqualify them from SSI and Medicaid — the most critical benefits they have.

**The solution:** A properly drafted SNT holds and distributes assets on behalf of your child without those assets counting against them for benefit eligibility purposes.

**What SNT funds can be used for:**
- Education and enrichment
- Recreation and travel
- Technology and equipment
- Therapy and medical expenses not covered by Medicaid
- Transportation
- Personal care items
- Virtually anything that enhances quality of life (NOT for food, shelter, or cash — this could affect SSI)

**How to fund the SNT:**
- Your estate/will/life insurance
- Direct contributions from relatives (better than direct gifts)
- Lawsuit settlements for your child
- Retirement account beneficiary designation

**Find a special needs attorney:** specialneedsalliance.org

### Age 18: The Transition Year

**Before the 18th birthday:**
- [ ] File for SSI in the month your child turns 18 (or up to 3 months before)
- [ ] Complete guardianship proceedings (if applicable)
- [ ] Apply for adult Medicaid (separate from child Medicaid in some states)
- [ ] Formally apply for Vocational Rehabilitation
- [ ] Register with Selective Service (required for males, also preserves federal benefit eligibility)
- [ ] Apply for adult HCBS waiver (if not already on list — get on MULTIPLE state waiver lists)

**SSI at 18 is different:** Parental income is NO LONGER counted. Even if your child was denied SSI as a child due to family income, apply again at 18. The disability test still applies, but many more individuals qualify.

**Expected SSI amount (2024):** Up to $943/month — potentially the beginning of financial independence.

---

### Ages 18–21: School Transition Services

Many states allow students to remain in public school through age 21 (or 22). During this time, the focus should be:
- **Employment:** Real, paid work experience in community settings
- **Independent living:** Cooking, cleaning, money management, transportation
- **Self-advocacy:** Knowing one's rights and how to ask for help
- **Community connections:** Building relationships outside of family

**Demand these from the transition IEP:**
- Community-based work experiences (not just school-based)
- Job coaching in real employment settings
- Life skills in real community settings (not simulated classroom)
- Post-secondary education exploration or enrollment
- Connection to adult services (attend meetings with VR, DD agency, etc.)

---

### Ages 21+: Life After School

**The adult services connection:**
- Medicaid waiver services should be activated by now (if you've been on the list since 12–14)
- Day program or supported employment should be in place
- Housing plan should be underway
- SSI and Medicaid should be active

**If services aren't in place:** Contact your state's DD agency, your state's Medicaid office, and VR immediately. Some families contact their state legislators' offices — local elected officials can sometimes accelerate access to services.

## PART 3: POST-SECONDARY EDUCATION OPTIONS

### For Students Who Can Access College

**Disability Services at Colleges and Universities**
Every college with federal funding must provide reasonable accommodations for students with documented disabilities. Students need to:
1. Self-disclose their disability (parents' role ends here unless student consents)
2. Submit documentation to disability services office
3. Request specific accommodations (extended time, note-takers, reduced course loads, etc.)

**Transition Support Programs at Colleges**
Many colleges now offer specialized programs with:
- Academic coaching
- Social skills support
- Housing support
- Employment readiness

Search: "autism college support programs" + your state

### Vocational Training Programs

For students who won't pursue traditional college:
- Community college certificate programs
- Trade schools and vocational training
- On-the-job training through supported employment
- Job training through Vocational Rehabilitation

### Specialized Transition Programs

Residential transition programs (often 2–4 years, ages 18–26) that focus on:
- Independent living skills
- Employment
- Social connections
- Community participation

Examples: College Living Experience (CLE), Threshold Program at Lesley University, Summit's College2Career

## PART 4: EMPLOYMENT

### The Employment Gap Is Real — And Addressable

Studies consistently show that 85%+ of adults with autism are unemployed or underemployed. This is not inevitable. Employment correlates with:
- Meaningful activity and purpose
- Social connection
- Mental health
- Financial independence

### Models of Employment

**Competitive integrated employment:** Regular job, regular pay, working alongside non-disabled coworkers. The gold standard.

**Supported employment:** Competitive integrated employment with a job coach providing ongoing support.

**Customized employment:** Job carved specifically around the individual's skills and needs, often part-time or with modified duties.

**Self-employment/microenterprise:** Small business built around the individual's strengths and interests.

### The Job Coach Role

A job coach:
- Helps identify appropriate jobs matching the individual's strengths
- Assists with applications, interviews, job training
- Works alongside the employee initially (job shadowing/training)
- Fades support as competence grows
- Available for ongoing consultation

**Funded by:** Vocational Rehabilitation initially, then Medicaid waiver for ongoing support

### Disclosure at Work

There is no legal requirement to disclose a disability at work unless requesting an accommodation. The ADA (Americans with Disabilities Act) protects qualified individuals with disabilities from employment discrimination.

**The accommodation request process:**
1. Notify employer (HR or direct supervisor) of the need for accommodation
2. Engage in an "interactive process" — conversation about what's needed
3. Employer must provide a reasonable accommodation unless it causes undue hardship

**Common autism-related workplace accommodations:**
- Private workspace or noise-canceling headphones
- Written instructions instead of verbal
- Flexible scheduling
- Modified break schedule
- Clearly defined expectations in writing
- Job coach support (especially initially)

## PART 5: HOUSING OPTIONS AND PLANNING

### Housing Decision Framework

Consider:
1. **Support needs:** How much daily support does your adult child need?
2. **Preference:** What does your child want?
3. **Resources:** What's available in your area? What can be funded?
4. **Long-term:** What happens when you can no longer provide support?

### Options at a Glance

**Living with family:** Many adults with autism live at home, often long-term. This works and is culturally normal in many communities. Plan for what happens if/when this changes.

**Supported living:** Adult lives in own apartment; staff comes in for agreed hours. Funds for staff hours through Medicaid waiver.

**Shared living:** Adult lives with a non-family member (host family or roommate) who provides support in exchange for payment (funded by Medicaid waiver).

**Group homes:** 4–6 residents with 24/7 staffing. Quality varies enormously — visit, ask questions, check licensing and inspection records.

**Independent living with natural supports:** Adult lives alone or with roommates, receives minimal formal support, relies on natural community connections.

### Starting the Housing Search

1. Contact your state's DD agency and ask specifically about housing options and funding
2. Contact local non-profit providers of residential services for adults with disabilities
3. Get on waitlists NOW — residential programs often have 5–10 year waits
4. Research Section 8 Housing Choice Vouchers for people with disabilities (longer waitlists, but worth applying)
5. Consult with a financial advisor specializing in disability planning
