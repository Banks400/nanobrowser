## WELCOME: WHY THIS GUIDE EXISTS

Every year, thousands of children with autism sit in classrooms with IEPs that don't actually meet their needs — not because the system is always malicious, but because parents don't know what to ask for, what they're entitled to, or how the process works.

The IEP (Individualized Education Program) is the single most powerful document in your child's educational life. It is legally binding. It determines what services your child receives, how many hours, in what setting, and measured against what goals. Getting it right — or getting it wrong — can change the trajectory of your child's development.

This guide gives you the knowledge, language, and strategy to walk into every IEP meeting as an equal partner — not a passive recipient of whatever the school decides to offer.

**What you'll learn:**
- Exactly what IDEA guarantees your child (federal law on your side)
- How to request evaluations, services, and placements the school may not volunteer
- The 13 required components of every legal IEP
- How to write (or push back on) measurable, meaningful IEP goals
- Scripts for difficult conversations with IEP teams
- What to do when the school says "no" — and how to escalate
- Extended School Year (ESY), Extended Time, and other critical provisions

## PART 1: YOUR LEGAL FOUNDATION

### The Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA)

IDEA is the federal law that gives your child the right to special education. It was last reauthorized in 2004 and provides specific, enforceable protections.

**Your child's six core rights under IDEA:**

**1. Free Appropriate Public Education (FAPE)**
Your child is entitled to special education and related services at no cost to you. "Appropriate" doesn't mean the best possible education — it means one that meets your child's unique needs and allows meaningful educational benefit. However, courts have increasingly interpreted "appropriate" as requiring more than minimal progress.

*2017 Endrew F. v. Douglas County: The Supreme Court ruled that FAPE requires "meaningful" progress, not merely "more than de minimis." Use this case in disputes.*

**2. Least Restrictive Environment (LRE)**
Your child must be educated alongside non-disabled peers to the maximum extent appropriate, with supplementary aids and services provided. Removal from regular education classes must be justified.

**3. Appropriate Evaluation**
Your child must be evaluated before placement in special education and before any significant change to their program. Evaluations must be:
- Comprehensive (covering all areas of suspected disability)
- Conducted by qualified professionals
- In the child's native language
- Completed within 60 days (federal; your state may require less)

**4. Individualized Education Program (IEP)**
Every eligible child must have an IEP developed, reviewed, and revised at least annually. You are a required member of the IEP team.

**5. Parent and Student Participation**
You have the right to participate in all decisions about your child's education, including identification, evaluation, and placement.

**6. Procedural Safeguards**
You have the right to inspect educational records, seek independent evaluations, participate in mediation, file state complaints, and request due process hearings.

### Section 504 vs. IDEA: What's the Difference?

| Feature | IDEA/IEP | Section 504 |
|---|---|---|
| Services provided | Yes (intensive) | Accommodations only |
| Qualifying standard | Eligibility categories + educational need | Any disability affecting major life activity |
| Written plan | Yes (IEP) | Yes (504 Plan) |
| Funding | Federal special education funds | No additional funding |
| When to use | When child needs specialized instruction | When child needs accommodations in regular class |

**For most children with autism:** IDEA/IEP is stronger and more comprehensive than Section 504. If your child has autism and attends a public school, pursue an IEP first.

## PART 2: THE EVALUATION PROCESS

### How to Request an Evaluation

You have the right to request an evaluation in writing at any time. The school cannot charge you for this evaluation.

**Evaluation Request Letter Template:**

> [Date]
> [Principal's Name]
> [School Name]
> [Address]
> 
> Dear [Principal's Name],
> 
> I am writing to formally request a comprehensive special education evaluation for my child, [Child's Name], date of birth [DOB], currently enrolled in [grade/class].
> 
> I am requesting this evaluation because [briefly describe concerns: difficulty with social interaction, communication, behavioral challenges, academic delays, etc.].
> 
> I understand that I will receive a Prior Written Notice and Parental Consent form within [your state's timeline] days. Please confirm receipt of this letter.
> 
> Sincerely,
> [Your name]
> [Contact information]

**Send via email AND certified mail with return receipt.** This creates a paper trail and starts the legal clock.

### What the Evaluation Should Include

For a child with autism, a comprehensive evaluation should include assessments of:

- **Cognitive/intellectual functioning** (IQ testing, if appropriate)
- **Academic achievement** (reading, math, writing)
- **Social-emotional and behavioral functioning**
- **Adaptive behavior** (daily living skills — Vineland, ABAS)
- **Communication** (expressive, receptive, pragmatic)
- **Occupational performance** (fine motor, sensory processing)
- **Speech and language** (articulation, language processing)
- **Autism-specific assessment** (ADOS-2, ADI-R if not recently done)

**If the school's evaluation doesn't cover all areas:** Request an Independent Educational Evaluation (IEE) at the district's expense. You have this right by law. They can refuse and take you to due process to defend their evaluation, but most schools will comply.

### Understanding Your Child's Evaluation Results

Key scores to know:

**Standard Scores:** Most tests use a scale where 100 is average. Scores of 85–115 are within the average range. Scores below 70–75 typically indicate a significant delay.

**Percentile Ranks:** A percentile rank of 25 means your child scored as well or better than 25% of peers. Below the 10th percentile (standard score ~80) typically indicates a significant need.

**Age Equivalents:** Be cautious with these — they're often misleading. A 7-year-old scoring at a "4-year-old level" doesn't mean they should receive 4-year-old instruction.

## PART 3: THE 13 REQUIRED IEP COMPONENTS

Every IEP must legally contain these 13 elements:

### 1. Present Levels of Academic Achievement and Functional Performance (PLAAFP)
The foundation of the IEP. Describes where your child is RIGHT NOW academically and functionally.

**What a strong PLAAFP includes:**
- Current assessment scores
- Specific, observable descriptions of current skills
- How the disability affects involvement in the general curriculum
- Strengths AND areas of need

**Red flag:** Vague PLAAFP language like "Johnny struggles with social skills" with no data. Push for specifics: "Johnny initiates peer interaction in 2 of 10 observed opportunities and requires adult prompting 80% of the time to maintain conversation."

### 2. Measurable Annual Goals
Goals must be measurable. "Johnny will improve his reading" is not measurable. "Johnny will read 60 words per minute from grade-level passages with 80% accuracy, as measured by curriculum-based reading probes, by [date]" IS measurable.

**The anatomy of a measurable IEP goal:**
- **Who:** The student
- **Will do what:** The observable behavior
- **Under what conditions:** Context/setting
- **To what degree:** Criteria for success (%, frequency, accuracy)
- **By when:** Timeline for achievement

**Examples of strong autism-specific IEP goals:**

*Communication:*
"[Student] will use a 3–4 word phrase to make a request in 4 out of 5 opportunities across 3 consecutive data collection periods, as measured by speech-language pathologist data."

*Social skills:*
"[Student] will initiate a social interaction with a peer (making a comment, asking a question, or offering to share) at least 2 times per 30-minute unstructured period, in 4 of 5 consecutive school days, as measured by teacher observation and data collection."

*Behavior:*
"[Student] will use a learned coping strategy (deep breathing, visual schedule reference, or verbal request for break) when experiencing frustration, with no more than 2 verbal prompts, in 8 of 10 observed opportunities, as measured by teacher behavioral data."

*Self-care:*
"[Student] will independently complete a 5-step morning routine (unpacking backpack, hanging up coat, putting work in folder, sitting at desk, beginning bell ringer activity) using a visual checklist without adult prompting, in 4 of 5 school days, as measured by teacher observation."

### 3. Short-Term Objectives or Benchmarks
Required for students who take alternate assessments. For others, best practice is to include them even when not required — they make progress more trackable.

### 4. Special Education and Related Services
Must specify:
- **Type** of service (speech therapy, ABA, OT, etc.)
- **Frequency** (3x/week, daily, etc.)
- **Duration** (30 minutes, 60 minutes)
- **Location** (classroom, therapy room, home)
- **Start date and anticipated end date**

**Common services for children with autism:**
- Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA) / Behavioral support
- Speech-Language Therapy (SLT)
- Occupational Therapy (OT)
- Physical Therapy (PT)
- Social skills training / social thinking curriculum
- Counseling/psychological services
- Assistive Technology (AAC devices, visual supports)
- Transportation to/from school

**Know these numbers:** Most speech therapy in schools is 30 minutes, 1–2x/week. Private speech therapy for autism is often 60 minutes, 3–5x/week. If your child's private therapist recommends more than the school offers, document that recommendation and bring it to the IEP meeting.

### 5. Participation with Non-Disabled Peers
The IEP must explain the extent to which your child will NOT participate in regular education and extracurricular activities — and why.

### 6. Accommodations and Modifications
**Accommodations** change HOW your child learns/demonstrates knowledge (extended time, preferential seating, frequent breaks, visual aids).

**Modifications** change WHAT your child is expected to learn (reduced work volume, alternate grade-level curriculum).

**Valuable accommodations for autism:**
- Extended time (1.5x or 2x) on all assignments and tests
- Preferential seating away from distractions
- Frequent movement breaks (every 20–30 minutes)
- Access to sensory tools (fidgets, weighted items)
- Visual schedule displayed at desk
- Advanced notice of schedule changes
- Reduced homework load
- Assignment modifications (shorter tests, oral instead of written)
- Use of word processor for writing tasks
- Noise-canceling headphones
- Reduced distraction testing environment
- Use of AAC device if applicable
- Social stories provided before new activities

### 7. State and District Assessments
Describes how your child will participate in standardized testing with accommodations, or whether they'll take an alternate assessment.

### 8. Dates and Location of Services
Start date, frequency, duration, and setting of all services must be specified.

### 9. Transition Plan (Age 16, or Earlier If Appropriate)
For students 16 and older, the IEP must include a transition plan addressing:
- Post-secondary education
- Vocational training
- Employment
- Independent living (if appropriate)

*See the companion guide "Transition to Adulthood Planner" for a complete breakdown.*

### 10. Age of Majority Notice (Age 17)
School must inform the student of rights that transfer at age 18.

### 11. Special Factors
IEP team must consider:
- Behavior issues: Does the child need a Behavioral Intervention Plan (BIP)?
- Limited English proficiency
- Braille needs (blindness/visual impairment)
- Communication needs (AAC, augmentative communication)
- Assistive technology needs

### 12. Measurable Postsecondary Goals (Age 16+)
Goals for after high school graduation.

### 13. Coordination of Transition Services (Age 16+)
Specific transition services needed to reach postsecondary goals.

## PART 4: PLACEMENT OPTIONS

The IEP team must determine the appropriate placement based on your child's needs, not on what the school has available.

### The Continuum of Placements

**Most Inclusive → Least Inclusive:**
1. **Full inclusion** in general education with supports/aides
2. **General education with resource room** pull-out (30–50% of time)
3. **Self-contained special education classroom** (full day in separate class)
4. **Separate special education school** (all special education)
5. **Residential school**
6. **Homebound/hospital program** (rare, for medical necessity)

**LRE principle:** Placement should be as inclusive as possible while still meeting the child's needs. The school cannot place a child in a more restrictive setting simply because it's more convenient or cheaper.

### How to Advocate for the Right Placement

1. Come with data from private evaluations and therapists
2. Request that the school's evaluation clearly justify its placement recommendation
3. If you disagree with placement, put your disagreement in writing at the IEP meeting
4. Request a Facilitated IEP or Mediation before filing for due process
5. Consider hiring a special education advocate (covered below)

## PART 5: THE IEP MEETING PLAYBOOK

### Before the Meeting: Preparation

**2–3 weeks before:**
- [ ] Request copies of all assessments and reports that will be discussed
- [ ] Review your child's current IEP and write down what's working and what's not
- [ ] Gather documentation from private providers (therapist reports, doctor letters)
- [ ] Write your own "parent vision statement" — where do you want your child to be in 1 year? 5 years?
- [ ] Prepare your questions and concerns in writing
- [ ] Consider bringing an advocate, support person, or trusted friend

**1 week before:**
- [ ] Confirm who will be at the meeting (request attendance list)
- [ ] Confirm the meeting will include all required team members
- [ ] Notify the school if you plan to record the meeting (required in some states)
- [ ] Prepare your list of requested services, goals, and accommodations

### At the Meeting: Tactics That Work

**Opening statement:** Start by sharing your positive parent vision.
> "Before we begin, I'd like to share my vision for [child's name]. I want [him/her] to [specific goal]. With the right support, I believe [he/she] can achieve this, and I'm here today as a partner to make sure [he/she] gets what [he/she] needs."

**When the school presents evaluation results:**
- Ask clarifying questions: "What does this score mean for his daily functioning?"
- Push for specifics: "Can you show me the data that supports this recommendation?"

**When a service is recommended that seems insufficient:**
> "I appreciate that recommendation, but [child's name]'s private therapist has recommended [X hours/week]. Can we discuss why there's a difference, and what data supports the school's recommendation?"

**When the school says services aren't available:**
> "I understand there may be logistics to work out, but I want to make sure we're not letting logistics drive what [child] needs. Can we document what [he/she] needs, and then figure out how to provide it?"

**When you disagree and need time:**
> "I'm not comfortable signing this today. I'd like to take it home, review it, and respond within the 10-day period. Is that possible?"

**You are NEVER required to sign an IEP at the meeting.** Take it home. Review it. Consult with an advocate if needed.

### After the Meeting: Critical Steps

- [ ] If you agreed to the IEP, sign it promptly so services can begin
- [ ] If you disagreed, put your disagreements in writing within 10 days
- [ ] Keep a copy of every IEP (keep a binder going back years)
- [ ] Track implementation — are services actually being delivered as written?
- [ ] Request progress reports at least as often as report cards are issued
- [ ] Schedule a 30-day check-in if new services are starting

## PART 6: WHEN THE SCHOOL SAYS "NO"

### Common Denials and How to Respond

**"We don't have that service."**
> "Under IDEA, you're required to provide what my child needs, not what you currently have. Can you explain how you plan to meet this need?"

**"Your child doesn't qualify."**
> "Can you provide me with the Prior Written Notice explaining your decision and the data that supports it?" (They are legally required to provide this.)

**"We don't use ABA in our schools."**
> "The courts have consistently found ABA to be an appropriate methodology for children with autism. I'd like this documented in the IEP, and I'd like to discuss why you're declining to use it."

**"That's not available in our district."**
> "Can you tell me how you'll meet this need through another method? If not, is the district prepared to fund placement in a program that can provide it?"

### Escalation Path

**Level 1: Document Everything**
Put every concern in writing. Follow up every verbal conversation with an email: "Per our conversation today, I understand that..."

**Level 2: Request Prior Written Notice**
After any decision you disagree with, request Prior Written Notice (PWN) in writing. The school must explain: (1) what they're doing or refusing to do, (2) why, (3) what data supports the decision.

**Level 3: Request Facilitated IEP**
Ask the district for a facilitated IEP meeting with a neutral third-party facilitator.

**Level 4: State Complaint**
File a complaint with your state's Department of Education if the school is violating procedural requirements of IDEA. Free, must be resolved in 60 days.

**Level 5: Mediation**
Voluntary, informal, free process with a neutral mediator. 70%+ of cases resolve at mediation.

**Level 6: Due Process Hearing**
Formal administrative hearing before an impartial hearing officer. You can represent yourself, hire a special education attorney, or use a non-attorney advocate.

**Cost consideration:** Special education attorneys charge $150–$400/hour. For significant disputes, this investment often pays off. Some attorneys work on contingency if they believe your case is strong (under IDEA's fee-shifting provisions, prevailing parents can recover attorney fees).

### Finding an Advocate
- Parent Training and Information (PTI) Center in your state: parentcenterhub.org
- Council of Parent Attorneys and Advocates (COPAA): copaa.org
- Wrightslaw (comprehensive special education law resource): wrightslaw.com

## PART 7: SPECIFIC AUTISM-RELATED IEP ISSUES

### Behavioral Intervention Plans (BIPs)

If your child's behavior impedes their learning or the learning of others, the IEP team must consider behavior strategies and supports.

**Functional Behavior Assessment (FBA):** Before writing a BIP, the school should conduct an FBA — a systematic process to understand the function (purpose) of the behavior. Most behaviors serve one of four functions:
1. To get something (attention, preferred item, sensory input)
2. To avoid something (work, sensory input, anxiety trigger)

A good BIP is based on the FBA and includes:
- Clear description of the target behavior
- Hypothesis about the function
- Antecedent strategies (prevent the behavior)
- Teaching replacement behaviors
- Consequence strategies (what to do when the behavior occurs)
- Crisis plan (if applicable)

**Red flag:** A BIP that is all punishment/consequence with no teaching of replacement skills. Push for a BIP that primarily focuses on teaching.

### Assistive Technology (AAC)

If your child has difficulty communicating verbally, the IEP team must consider assistive technology. For non-speaking or minimally verbal children, this means Augmentative and Alternative Communication (AAC).

**Types of AAC:**
- Low-tech: PECS (Picture Exchange Communication System), communication boards
- Mid-tech: Speech-generating devices (SGDs), iPads with communication apps (Proloquo2Go, TouchChat, LAMP)
- High-tech: Dynamic display SGDs

**Your rights:** If your child's speech-language pathologist recommends an AAC device, the school must provide it for educational use. If it's recommended by a private SLP for use across environments, insurance and/or Medicaid may cover it.

### Extended School Year (ESY)

ESY is continued special education services during summer and school breaks. This is not the same as summer school — it's the continuation of IEP services.

**Who qualifies:** Children who would experience significant regression during breaks and who wouldn't recoup those skills within a reasonable time when school resumes.

**Your school likely will not automatically offer ESY.** You must ask, and you must document regression. Start keeping data on what your child loses over winter break, spring break, and summer. Bring that data to the IEP meeting and request ESY.

## IEP ADVOCACY TOOLKIT

### Questions to Ask at Every IEP Meeting
1. How many hours per week of each service is my child receiving currently?
2. What data is being used to make this recommendation?
3. How will progress toward each goal be measured and reported?
4. What happens if my child isn't making adequate progress?
5. What additional supports could be added if current supports aren't working?
6. Is my child receiving all services that were agreed to in last year's IEP?
7. How is my child doing socially with peers?
8. Are there any behaviors that need to be addressed in a BIP?

### Red Flags in an IEP
- Goals that aren't measurable ("will improve...")
- No baseline data supporting PLAAFP
- Services listed without specific frequency/duration
- Placement decided before evaluation is complete
- Meeting starts with the IEP already written and just needs your signature
- Team pressure to sign the IEP at the meeting
- No discussion of transition planning for a 16+ year old

### Your Parental Rights Summary
- Right to participate in IEP meetings
- Right to copies of all educational records (within 45 days of request)
- Right to prior written notice of any proposed changes
- Right to withhold consent for evaluation or placement changes
- Right to independent educational evaluation at district expense
- Right to file a state complaint (free, resolved in 60 days)
- Right to request mediation (free, voluntary)
- Right to due process hearing
- Right to bring anyone you choose to IEP meetings
- Right to have an interpreter if English is not your primary language

## ANNUAL IEP TIMELINE PLANNER

**3 months before annual review:**
- Review current IEP — what worked, what didn't
- Request updated assessment data from all providers
- Collect progress monitoring data from school
- Begin drafting your parent input/vision statement

**6 weeks before:**
- Request evaluation results and draft IEP if possible
- Contact private providers for updated reports
- Schedule pre-meeting with teacher/case manager if needed

**2 weeks before:**
- Review all documents received
- Prepare your questions and requests in writing
- Identify non-negotiables vs. negotiable points
- Consider consulting an advocate if significant changes are proposed

**At the meeting:**
- Follow the IEP meeting playbook (Part 5 above)
- Take notes or record (check state law)
- Don't feel pressured to sign

**After the meeting:**
- Monitor service delivery (are sessions actually happening?)
- Track progress on goals monthly
- Address concerns in writing immediately
